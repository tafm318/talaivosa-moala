I am a farmer on a 1.5-acre market garden. I can afford to plant 64 beds total of tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun with the help of 4 temporary workers. There is a bed caps sum of 70: 20 beds of tomatoes, 20 beds of carrots, and 30 beds of mesclun. I cannot max out on every crop, so I need decide which crop must give. Other factors to consider are revenue per bed, field hours per week per bed, fertilizer per bed, and diminishing-returns rate. Below is a table highlighting the factors for each crop. 

Crop | Bed Cap | Revenue/Bed | Field HRS/WK/Bed | Fertilizer/Bed | Diminishing-Returns Rate |
|---|---|---|
Tomatoes | 20 beds | $8,800 | 2.5 | $880 | 10% |
Carrots | 20 beds | $2,094 | 0.833 | $440 | 2.5% |
Mesclun | 30 beds | $2,700 | 1.25 | $880 | 1.25% |

Tomatoes earn the most per bed at $8,800 compared to $2,700 for mesclun and $2,094 for carrots, which pulls toward planting more tomatoes. But tomatoes also have a 10% compounding rate compared to 2.5% for carrots and 1.25% for mesclun, which pushes the other way by making each extra bed more labor-hungry. My hypothesis is where those two forces meet is the profit-maximizing number of tomato beds. 

I expect tomatoes to stop because rising marginal cost catches the flat price - the 10% compounding makes labor cost rise too fast to keep expanding. I expect carrots and mesclun to stop because they hit their bed caps - at 2.5% and 1.25% their costs stay low, so price still covers cost when they run out of beds. 

If tomatoes come in below 15 beds, the compounding penalty bites much earlier than I thought.
